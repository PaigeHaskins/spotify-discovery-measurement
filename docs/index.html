First, setting aside what I slept through

I fall asleep to brown noise and let background playlists run for hours, so before measuring discovery I set aside 1,972 hours (25% of all listening).

Two rules decide what counts as background. By content: 554 artists whose album or track titles are mostly sleep or noise phrases (brown noise, rain sounds, sleep jazz). By behavior: 270 sessions where autoplay ran for hours with almost no skips or clicks, which happens when no one is listening; every play in them is set aside, and 821 artists heard almost only in those sessions are removed entirely. Left in, they would have skewed the results: set-aside artists "came back" 40% of the time, because the same sleep sounds play night after night.

How much am I discovering, and how much sticks?

I met 2,929 new artists. Of those with a full 30-day window to observe, 24% earned a return visit on a later day. That return rate is the conversion metric the rest of this page tries to predict and explain.

Can the first listen predict which artists stick?

Yes. On discoveries the model never saw, it ranks future returners above non-returners 0.60 of the time (95% CI 0.54 to 0.66; a coin flip scores 0.50).

If I promoted only the top 10% of new artists by predicted score, 36% of them would come back, versus 19% overall: a 1.9x lift. That is the campaign-targeting framing: the model's value is in who it puts at the top of the list.

Evaluation details

Trained on 2,185 discoveries (2017-01-16 to 2024-02-06), tested on the next 729 (2024-02-07 to 2026-08-25). Splitting by time, not at random, mirrors how a production model predicts the future from the past. Does it matter how I find an artist?

Artists that simply played next came back 5.3 percentage points less often than artists I clicked on in comparable situations (95% CI -11.9 to -0.8).

Passive means the song started because the previous one ended (autoplay, radio, queue); active means I clicked that exact song. Discovery Mode lives in the passive lane, which is why this split matters. The raw gap was -9.4 pts; part of it came from when and where passive listening happens, not from the path itself. This compares the 72% of passive discoveries that had a close active counterpart; the rest happened in situations where I almost never clicked a song myself, so there is nothing fair to compare them with.

Method and diagnostics

A logistic model estimated each discovery's chance of being passive from context known before the song finished: time of day, weekend, device, shuffle, offline, year, and a time trend. Each passive discovery was compared only with active discoveries from the same year, on the same kind of device, in the same part of the day, on the same kind of day (weekday or weekend), using up to 5 with the closest score within a caliper of 0.2 SD (995 of 1,381 passive discoveries matched). Listen length and same-day replays were deliberately left out: they happen after the path is set, so adjusting for them would hide part of the effect.

Balance: every covariate ended inside the ±0.1 band. Confidence intervals come from 500 bootstrap resamples of the whole procedure.

Matching only balances what was measured. Mood, activity, or a friend's recommendation are not in the data, so this is evidence, not proof. The coin-flip experiment below is the design that removes that gap.

Testing it with a coin flip

The experiment is designed and ready to start. Create the randomized schedule with: python -m src.experiment plan

The matched comparison above can only adjust for what was recorded. To remove that limitation, days are randomized in pairs: a coin flip decides which day of each pair is Smart Shuffle on and which is Normal listening. The outcome, new artists that day, comes straight from the streaming export, and the analysis follows the assignment even on days I forgot (intention to treat).

Before starting: my history averages 0.9 new artists a day. The table shows the smallest true change each experiment length can reliably detect (5% false-positive rate, 80% power).

How this was measured

Set aside
Sleep and background listening: artists whose titles are mostly sleep or noise phrases, and every play in a session that ran 3+ hours (or 60+ minutes starting at night) with skips or clicks on under 5% of tracks. 220,457 of 256,881 plays remain for analysis.
Stream
A play of at least 30 seconds. Shorter plays count as skips.
Discovery
The first stream of an artist, excluding the first 90 days of history, where most "new" artists were already familiar.
Return (conversion)
Any stream of that artist on a later calendar day within 30 days. Discoveries in the final 30 days are counted but not labeled, because their window is not over.
Data
256,881 music plays from 2016-10-18 to 2026-09-28, loaded from Spotify's extended export. Personal identifiers (IP address, user agent) were dropped on load. Times are in America/New_York.
Pipeline
Python loads the JSON; SQL models (staging, intermediate, marts) build the tables; 17 automated data-quality checks recompute key numbers a second way before anything is published.

Limitations: one listener, so none of this generalizes to Spotify's users. Artists are matched by name because the export has no artist ID. The export says how a song started, not which playlist or algorithm served it, so this measures discovery behavior, not Spotify's recommendation systems.
